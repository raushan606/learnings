Why a normal database drowns

Imagine every server, pod, and JVM in your fleet reporting a few hundred numbers every 15 seconds, forever. That's the workload: a relentless stream of measurements, almost all writes, always in time order, and later queried in ranges ("CPU over the last 6 hours") or aggregates ("p99 latency per service").

A general-purpose database handles this badly — a row per sample means billions of tiny rows, indexes that balloon, and slow range scans. A time-series database (TSDB) is purpose-built for exactly this shape, and it makes very different trade-offs to win on cost and speed.

This guide answers four practical questions, each a knob you'll actually tune: how data gets in (pull vs push), how it's stored (the internals), how you keep it affordable as it ages (downsampling), and how long you keep it (retention). VictoriaMetrics — the engine behind a lot of modern observability stacks — is the concrete example throughout.

01 / Foundation

The shape of the data

In plain words

A metric isn't one number — it's a named line on a graph, split out by labels. http_requests_total{method="GET"} and http_requests_total{method="POST"} are two separate lines. Each line collects a steady drip of timestamped readings.

Three words to know

A series is a metric name plus a set of labels — one unique line. A sample is a single (timestamp, value) reading on that line. And cardinality is the total number of distinct series — the single biggest driver of cost, which we'll return to at the end.

In VictoriaMetrics

The full label set is hashed to a compact numeric id called a TSID. From then on, samples are stored against that id, and a separate index (IndexDB) maps labels back to it — that's how a query like {status="200"} finds the right series fast.

02 / Getting data in

Pull vs push

In plain words

Two ways to collect the mail. Pull: the database drives to each house on a schedule and picks up the mail (it scrapes each target's /metrics page). Push: each house mails its letters in whenever it likes (apps and agents send samples to the database).

Why the choice matters

Pull gives the collector control: it decides what to scrape and how often, and a target that stops responding is instantly visible as "down" — great for a fleet you can enumerate. Push suits things the collector can't reach or can't list ahead of time: short-lived batch jobs, serverless functions, mobile/IoT devices, or anything behind a firewall. The cost is that you must trust and rate-limit whatever is sending.

In VictoriaMetrics

It does both. For pull, you run vmagent to scrape Prometheus-style /metrics endpoints. For push, it accepts a long list of protocols — Prometheus remote_write, OpenTelemetry, InfluxDB line protocol, Graphite, OpenTSDB, DataDog/StatsD, and more. Same database, many front doors.

# PULL: vmagent scrapes these targets every 15s and forwards them
scrape_configs:
  - job_name: payara
    scrape_interval: 15s
    static_configs:
      - targets: ["payara-1:8080", "payara-2:8080"]
# PUSH: send samples straight in (Prometheus text format here)
curl -X POST http://victoria:8428/api/v1/import/prometheus \
     --data-binary 'payara_heap_used_bytes{node="1"} 734003200'
03 / The engine room

Storage internals

In plain words

The trick to storing a firehose cheaply: never edit anything. New samples get buffered in memory, then written out as small, immutable files. Background jobs later merge those files into bigger, tidier ones — like emptying your inbox into neatly filed, sealed folders you never reopen.

The write path

When a sample arrives, its labels are resolved to a numeric id, and the (id, timestamp, value) triple lands in an in-memory buffer. When the buffer fills (or on a timer), it's flushed to disk as a part — a self-contained, immutable directory of files. Reads and writes never fight over the same bytes, which is what keeps ingestion fast.

Columnar layout & compression

Inside a part, timestamps and values live in separate files, each holding blocks of same-series samples. Storing one kind of number together makes it compress astonishingly well, because consecutive values look alike: timestamps are usually a fixed step apart, and a counter barely changes between readings.

Most TSDBs lean on Gorilla-style compression (delta-encode timestamps, XOR successive floats). VictoriaMetrics adds its own tricks — turning floats into integers where it can, delta-encoding counters — and then runs general-purpose ZSTD on top. The payoff, per its docs, is roughly 0.4 bytes per data point for typical metrics, versus about 1.5 for Prometheus and ~4 for raw Gorilla.

The label index

Samples answer "what was the value?"; the inverted index answers "which series match these labels?". It maps every label=value to the series carrying it, so a query filters down to matching series first, then reads only their blocks. This index is powerful but not free — its size grows with the number of series and the length of their labels, which is exactly where cardinality bites (section 07).

In VictoriaMetrics

Data is split into per-month partitions; within them, parts hold blocks of up to ~8,192 rows per series. There's no write-ahead log — durability comes from the storage design, and at most a few seconds of not-yet-flushed data is at risk on a hard crash. Background merges keep the parts tidy, and the label index lives in a sibling indexdb directory.

04 / Keeping it affordable

Downsampling

In plain words

You don't need per-second detail from last year. Downsampling replaces old, fine-grained samples with coarser summaries — say one point every 5 minutes instead of every 15 seconds. You keep the shape of history while throwing away detail nobody queries, so storage and long-range queries both get much cheaper.

What it does

Downsampling aggregates many raw samples into one per larger time bucket — typically keeping an average, min, max, or the last value per bucket. A dashboard showing "the last year" doesn't want a billion points anyway; a few thousand summarized points draw the same trend far faster.

In VictoriaMetrics

Built-in multi-level downsampling (-downsampling.period=offset:interval) is an Enterprise feature. In the open-source build you get the same effect two ways: stream aggregation in vmagent (aggregate samples before they're stored — also a StatsD replacement), or recording rules in vmalert that periodically compute rollups with MetricsQL. One caveat: downsampling reduces samples per series, but it does not reduce the number of series, so it won't shrink the label index.

# Enterprise: raw for 30d, 5-min points to 180d, 1-hour points beyond
victoria-metrics -retentionPeriod=2y \
  -downsampling.period=30d:5m \
  -downsampling.period=180d:1h
# Open source: vmagent stream aggregation — one summary per 5 minutes
- interval: 5m
  match: '{__name__=~".+"}'
  outputs: [avg, max]        # what to keep per 5-minute bucket
05 / Keeping it bounded

Retention

In plain words

Retention is your "keep for N days, then bin it" rule. Without it, a metrics database grows forever. With it, data past the cutoff is deleted automatically, so disk usage settles into a predictable ceiling.

Because a TSDB stores data in time-ordered partitions, deleting old data is cheap: it doesn't hunt through billions of rows, it just drops whole partitions once they fall entirely outside the retention window. This is where downsampling and retention team up — the two together define your data's whole lifecycle.

In VictoriaMetrics

Retention is one flag: -retentionPeriod. The default is 1 month (31 days), the minimum is 1 day, and older data is removed automatically at partition boundaries. Setting different retention for different datasets (e.g. keep security metrics longer than debug metrics) is an Enterprise feature.

victoria-metrics -storageDataPath=/var/lib/vm -retentionPeriod=12M
# keep 12 months; anything older is dropped a partition at a time
06 / Putting it together

VictoriaMetrics, assembled

Now the concepts map onto real components. The single-node build packs everything into one small binary — perfect to start with. The cluster build splits the job into independent, shared-nothing services so you can scale writes and reads separately.

Reading the diagram against the earlier sections: vmagent is where pull (scraping) and push meet, and where stream-aggregation downsampling happens. vminsert spreads writes evenly across storage nodes. vmstorage is the engine from section 03 — parts, columnar files, the index, merges — and it owns -retentionPeriod. vmselect runs your PromQL/MetricsQL queries by fanning out to every storage node and merging the results.

07 / The thing that actually bites

In practice: cardinality

In plain words

The number that decides whether your TSDB stays cheap or falls over usually isn't how often you sample — it's how many distinct series exist. Every unique combination of labels is a new line to track and index. Add one label that takes thousands of values, and you've quietly multiplied your series count by thousands.

Why labels are the trap

A well-behaved label has a small, bounded set of values — an HTTP method has a handful, a status code a few dozen. A dangerous label is unbounded or high-churn: a user id, a request id, a UUID, or anything derived from the current time. In Kubernetes this happens by accident — every pod restart mints a fresh pod label, so the same metric spawns endless short-lived series, and the label index can grow to larger than the data itself.

Watch out

Downsampling and longer retention won't rescue a cardinality problem — they shrink samples, not the number of series, so the index keeps growing. The real fixes are upstream: drop or coarsen high-churn labels with relabeling, pre-aggregate with stream aggregation, and hunt down the worst offenders with a cardinality explorer before they hurt.

In VictoriaMetrics

The built-in cardinality explorer shows which metrics and labels generate the most series. Pair it with relabeling (drop noisy labels at ingestion) and stream aggregation (roll many short-lived series into a few stable ones). Watching active-series count and indexdb size over time is one of the highest-signal habits in running a metrics stack.
