The problem: the model doesn't know your stuff

A language model only knows what it saw during training. It has never read your company wiki, last week's tickets, or that 300-page policy PDF. You could retrain it on your documents — but that's slow, expensive, and out of date the moment a document changes.

There's a much cheaper move: when a question comes in, find the few passages that answer it and paste them into the prompt. The model reads them right there and answers from them. That pattern is called RAG — Retrieval-Augmented Generation: retrieve the right text, then let the model generate an answer using it.

Everything in this guide serves that one move. Two ideas do the heavy lifting: embeddings turn meaning into numbers so a computer can compare it, and vector search uses those numbers to find the passages closest in meaning to a question.

01 / Meaning as numbers

Embeddings (a quick review)

In plain words

Picture a map where location means meaning. On this map, "cat" and "kitten" sit right next to each other; "cat" and "bicycle" are far apart. An embedding model reads a piece of text and hands you its coordinates on that map — a list of numbers that says where this meaning lives.

What it is

An embedding is a fixed-length list of numbers (a vector, often several hundred or a thousand values) that represents the meaning of a piece of text. The key property: similar meanings produce nearby vectors, and different meanings produce distant ones.

Why it matters

Computers can't compare meaning directly, but they're great at comparing numbers. Embeddings turn the fuzzy question "how similar are these two texts?" into simple arithmetic on two vectors. That single trick is what makes semantic search, recommendations, and RAG possible.

How to do it with Claude

Anthropic doesn't ship its own embedding model — instead it recommends Voyage AI as the embeddings provider. Voyage offers general models (like voyage-3.5 and voyage-3-large), a cheaper voyage-3.5-lite, and domain-tuned models for finance, law, and code. One thing worth knowing: set input_type to "document" when embedding your passages and "query" when embedding a question — it improves retrieval quality.

import voyageai
vo = voyageai.Client()   # reads VOYAGE_API_KEY from your env

docs = ["Refunds are processed within 5 business days.",
        "Our office is closed on public holidays."]

doc_vecs = vo.embed(docs, model="voyage-3.5",
                    input_type="document").embeddings   # list of vectors
Where you'd use it
  • Semantic search — find text by meaning, not exact keywords.
  • Recommendations — "more like this" by vector nearness.
  • Clustering & deduplication — group texts that mean the same thing.
02 / Scoring closeness

Cosine similarity

In plain words

Think of each embedding as an arrow pointing out from the center. Cosine similarity measures the angle between two arrows — not how long they are. Pointing the same way → about 1 (very similar). At a right angle → 0 (unrelated). Pointing opposite → −1.

What it is

Cosine similarity is a single number from −1 to 1 that says how aligned two vectors are. Because it only cares about direction, it ignores how "big" a vector is — so a short sentence and a long paragraph about the same topic still score as similar.

The formula, gently

Cosine similarity is the dot product of the two vectors divided by the product of their lengths. You rarely compute it by hand — but here it is in code, plus a handy shortcut:

import numpy as np

def cosine(a, b):
    a, b = np.array(a), np.array(b)
    return a @ b / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))

# Shortcut: if vectors are already normalized (length 1),
# the dot product `a @ b` IS the cosine — no division needed.
Good to know

Vector databases usually let you pick the distance metric — cosine, dot product, or Euclidean. For text embeddings, cosine is the common default, and you'll see it everywhere in RAG.

03 / Finding the nearest passages

Vector search

In plain words

You've turned every passage into a point on the meaning map. To answer a question, you turn the question into a point too, then look for the nearest points — those passages are the most relevant. That's the whole idea of vector search: nearest neighbors by meaning.

What it is

Given a query vector, vector search returns the stored vectors closest to it (highest cosine similarity). For a small collection you can just compare against every vector — that's "brute force" and it's perfectly fine for thousands of items.

q = vo.embed(["how long do refunds take?"], model="voyage-3.5",
             input_type="query").embeddings[0]

scores = [cosine(q, v) for v in doc_vecs]   # score every stored chunk
best_i = int(np.argmax(scores))
print(docs[best_i])                          # -> the refunds sentence

When it gets big

Comparing against millions of vectors one by one is too slow. That's where vector databases come in — tools like Pinecone, Weaviate, Qdrant, Chroma, Milvus, or Postgres with pgvector. They use approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) indexes that find the closest vectors almost instantly, trading a sliver of accuracy for a huge speed-up. They also store your metadata and handle filtering (next up).

Where you'd use it
  • The retrieval step of any RAG system.
  • "Find similar documents / products / images" features.
  • Detecting near-duplicate or anomalous text at scale.
04 / Slicing your documents

Chunking strategies

In plain words

You can't hand the model a whole 300-page manual for every question. You slice it into bite-size passages — chunks — so retrieval can pull just the relevant slices. How you slice matters a lot: it's often the single biggest lever on how good your RAG system feels.

Why size matters

Chunks that are too big mix several topics, which muddies the embedding and wastes room in the prompt. Chunks that are too small chop ideas in half and lose the context needed to be useful. The goal is chunks that each hold roughly one coherent idea.

The main strategies

Fixed size

Cut every N tokens or characters. Dead simple, but can slice mid-sentence.

easiest · lowest quality

With overlap

Repeat a little text between chunks (say 10–20%) so boundary-spanning ideas survive.

a cheap, reliable win

By sentence / paragraph

Split on natural breaks so each chunk is a whole thought, not a fragment.

keeps ideas intact

Structure-aware

Split on headings, sections, or (for code) functions. Great when docs have real structure.

best for structured docs

Semantic

Group sentences by similarity so each chunk covers one topic. Smarter, a bit more work.

topic-tight chunks

Rule of thumb

Start ~a few hundred tokens with small overlap, respect natural boundaries, then measure.

where to begin
Tip

There's no universal best chunk size. Anthropic's own advice for RAG is plain: experiment with chunk size, boundary, and overlap, then evaluate on your own data. Chunking is where you should spend real tuning effort.

05 / Narrowing the search

Metadata filtering

In plain words

Cosine similarity finds passages that sound relevant. Metadata is the label on each passage — its source, date, author, product, language, or who's allowed to see it. Filtering by metadata is like telling the librarian "search only the 2024 finance folder" before you go looking for the closest book.

What it is

When you store each chunk's vector, you also store a few plain fields alongside it — its metadata. At query time you use those fields to restrict the search to the chunks that qualify, then rank the survivors by meaning.

Why it matters

Three reasons. Relevance: a perfect meaning-match from the wrong year is still the wrong answer. Correctness: keep results inside the right scope (this product, this region). Security: filtering by user_id or access level stops one customer's documents from surfacing in another's results.

# store each chunk WITH metadata
collection.add(
    documents=chunks,
    embeddings=chunk_vecs,
    metadatas=[{"source": "policy", "year": 2024, "team": "finance"}, ...],
)

# restrict to 2024 finance policy, THEN rank the matches by meaning
results = collection.query(
    query_embeddings=[q],
    n_results=3,
    where={"year": 2024, "source": "policy", "team": "finance"},
)
Related: hybrid search

Meaning search can miss exact strings. Ask for "Error code TS-999" and an embedding may return general error-handling text but skip the precise match. Hybrid search pairs semantic search with old-fashioned keyword search (BM25) so exact terms — codes, names, IDs — still land. You'll see this again in the next section.

Where you'd use it
  • Multi-tenant apps that must keep each customer's data separate.
  • Time-sensitive answers where only the latest version counts.
  • Large knowledge bases split by product, region, or department.
06 / Putting it together

The RAG pipeline

Now the pieces click together. A RAG system runs in two phases: an index phase you run once (and refresh when docs change), and a query phase that runs on every question.

Here's the whole thing as one small program — embed a corpus, find the closest chunks by cosine, stuff them into the prompt, and let Claude answer. Notice the guardrail in the system prompt (answer only from the context, or say you don't know) — the same idea from the prompt-engineering chapter.

import voyageai, numpy as np
from anthropic import Anthropic

vo, claude = voyageai.Client(), Anthropic()

# --- index time (run once) ---
chunks = [ ... ]                                   # your document passages
vecs = vo.embed(chunks, model="voyage-3.5",
                input_type="document").embeddings

def cosine(a, b):
    a, b = np.array(a), np.array(b)
    return a @ b / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))

# --- query time (per question) ---
question = "What's our refund window?"
qv = vo.embed([question], model="voyage-3.5",
              input_type="query").embeddings[0]

top = sorted(range(len(chunks)),
             key=lambda i: cosine(qv, vecs[i]), reverse=True)[:3]
context = "\n\n".join(chunks[i] for i in top)      # the retrieved passages

msg = claude.messages.create(
    model="claude-sonnet-4-5",
    max_tokens=512,
    system=("Answer using ONLY the context below. "
            "If the answer isn't there, say you don't know."),   # guardrail
    messages=[{"role": "user",
        "content": f"<context>\n{context}\n</context>\n\nQ: {question}"}],
)
print(msg.content[0].text)

Retrieve by meaning, then generate from the retrieved text — that's RAG in one screen.

07 / Level up

Contextual retrieval (Anthropic's upgrade)

In plain words

Here's a sneaky failure. A chunk says "Revenue grew 3% over the previous quarter" — but the company name and the quarter were mentioned two paragraphs earlier, in a different chunk. On its own, this chunk barely matches the question "What was ACME's Q2 2024 growth?", so it gets buried. Naive chunking throws away context, and that's the number-one reason RAG returns "I couldn't find it."

The fix

Anthropic's Contextual Retrieval is simple: before embedding each chunk, prepend a short context line (generated by Claude) that situates the chunk in its document — the company, the section, the time period. You embed and index that enriched chunk. Pair it with keyword (BM25) search for a hybrid system that catches exact terms too.

# ask Claude to situate each chunk in its document, then embed THIS
prompt = f"""Here is a document:
<document>{whole_document}</document>
Here is one chunk from it:
<chunk>{chunk}</chunk>
In 1-2 sentences, say what this chunk is about and where it fits,
so it's easier to find by search. Reply with that context only."""

context_line = claude.messages.create(...).content[0].text
chunk_to_embed = context_line + "\n\n" + chunk        # embed + index this

Does it help? (measured)

Anthropic's own tests report real gains in retrieval accuracy (they measure the rate of failed retrievals):

Reported improvements
  • Contextual embeddings alone — about 35% fewer failed retrievals (5.7% → 3.7%).
  • Adding contextual keyword (BM25) search — about 49% fewer.
  • Adding a reranking step on top — about 67% fewer.
Two more terms you'll meet

Reranking: retrieve a wide net (say the top 150 chunks), then a reranker model re-scores them and you keep the best few (say 20). Prompt caching: generating a context line per chunk costs tokens — caching the shared document keeps that cheap. And always test on your data; published numbers don't automatically transfer.
